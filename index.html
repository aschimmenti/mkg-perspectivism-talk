Discourses where positionality matters

politicsmuseum artefactsdescriptionsattributionsmeasurement and analysis

An MKG should hold multiple perspectives. Annotating perspective makes potential bias explicit, in interpretative and in factual statements alike. In perspectivism an agent looks at an object from a stance; provenance tells us who the agent is.

Take a factual statement about a museum artefact. “Acquired” and “looted” describe the same event from different positions. The stance is rarely recorded: a source gives evidence, seldom the positionality behind it.

A harder case from Wikidata. Jerusalem is simply asserted and both Tel Aviv statements are deprecated; the choice matches the US position (December 2017). The qualifiers record who disputes the claim. From which positionality the claim was made stays unsaid.

Claims and their positionality join into discourses (Foucault): the statements and rules that decide what can be said about a subject. Positionality matters in many of them, and even naming one is a stance: colonialism in Palestine, or the Zionist discourse?

An MKG should hold multiple perspectives, which presupposes we know what a perspective is and can annotate it with some confidence. Annotating it makes potential bias explicit, for facts and plain triples too.

Perspectivism (Nietzsche, Ortega y Gasset, Giere): an agent sees an object from a stance, like a painter whose canvas depends on where they stand and why. Provenance (as in PROV-O) is attached to the agent. The stance has no place.

Benin Bronzes: “acquired” or “looted” in 1897, same event, different positions. If the source says why, provenance can carry it; otherwise we must infer it, reliably and at scale.

Wikidata, capital of Israel: Jerusalem simply asserted, two Tel Aviv statements deprecated. Qualifiers record who disputes the claim, but not the positionality it was made from. The choice matches the US position (Dec 2017); most UN member states hold the other view (UNGA ES-10/19).

Claims and their positionality join into discourses (Foucault). Positionality matters in politics, museum artefacts, descriptions, attributions, and in how something is measured or analysed. Even naming a discourse is a stance.

2 · Suggested principles and critical review

A statement should be annotated with its provenance and its positionality

Annotating stance and positionality is always possible: it depends on what the source tells us.

Annotating statements with stance, perspective and positionality is always possible. The question is how, and at scale.

When the source says so, it is easy: the declared stance goes into provenance.

When it does not, we infer it from variables such as method, background and discourse. That inference is another interpretation, and it can be biased by our own positionality.

Existing models, provenance-based and epistemic, tell us what was claimed and by whom. None says from which stance.

A constructivist route. First, take the claim as stated: “the novel lays bare the class order behind its characters”.

Trace its provenance: who wrote it, where and when.

Analyse the agent: their method, background and other claims.

Perspectivize: propose a candidate stance, here Marxist, from those variables and the discourse the claim belongs to.

Signature: the KG maintainer declares its own stance on the annotation.

Annotating statements with stance, perspective and positionality is always possible. Two cases. A declared stance (the painter says why) goes in provenance. An attributed stance is inferred from variables, and that inference is another interpretation, open to our own biases.

Existing models, provenance (PROV-O, nanopublications, named graphs) and epistemic ones (CRMinf, DEC, DOLCE-Epistemic, Conjectures, Situations), record who, when and how. None has a slot for why, and the epistemic ones need the reasoning to be documented already.

A constructivist route: the claim; its provenance; analysis of method and background of the agents; perspectivizing the claim, i.e. attributing a candidate perspective from variables such as method, background and discourse; and provenance for the annotation itself, from the KG maintainer. At some level the knowledge base has to be transparent about its own stance.

3 · Discussion for breakout groups

Perspectives as first-class entities?

  1. What identifies a perspective?
  2. Who may declare one?
  3. Who annotates the annotator?
One possibility, for fun

Should a perspective be an entity of its own, linked to the claims that belong to it? Example: every Marxist claim about literary entities in novels.

Open points: what identifies a perspective, who may declare one, and who annotates the annotator. Attributing a stance is itself a claim that can be contested.

One possibility: gather claims with their provenance into a named graph and read it as a discourse, then infer a stance from it. The catch: choosing which claims belong to the discourse already assumes the stance.

Slide 3 · about 60 s

The question for the groups: should perspectives be first-class entities in an MKG, and how? Use case: all Marxist claims about literary entities in novels. Declared Marxist readings are easy; the rest would be attributed.

Open points: what identifies a perspective; who may declare one; the trade-off that attributing stances gives coverage while each attribution is itself a claim that can be contested; who annotates the annotator.

If time remains: put claims with provenance into a named graph, read the graph as a discourse, link a stance to it, derived from the graph. The catch: the graph is defined by its claims and the stance is inferred from the graph, so the argument is circular.
